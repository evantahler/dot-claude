# sound-human

This skill stops Claude from sounding like Claude when the deliverable is prose someone will read as content.

The 2024 tells everyone learned to spot ("delve," em-dashes, "tapestry") are mostly trained out. The 2026 tells are different and more structural: negative parallelism, rule-of-three, sensory metaphors stuck to abstractions, tidy summary conclusions, symmetrical paragraphs, the sycophantic opener, the safe consensus middle. Vocabulary alone won't fix it — sentence architecture and stance have to change too.

Treat this as a two-stage workflow: **constrain before drafting**, then **audit after drafting**. The constraints prevent the worst defaults; the audit catches what slips through.

---

## When to apply this skill

Apply it when the output is content — something a person will read as a finished or near-finished piece of writing. That includes:

- Blog posts, articles, essays, op-eds
- Marketing copy, landing pages, launch announcements, ad copy
- Technical writing, documentation, tutorials, README sections, changelogs written for humans
- Social posts (LinkedIn, X, Threads, Bluesky), newsletter sections
- Email and outreach (cold or warm), pitches, intros
- Ghostwritten work in someone else's voice
- Fiction, short stories, scripts

Do NOT apply it to: inline conversational answers in a chat, code, JSON or other structured output, short factual replies, math, or anything where the user wants the answer rather than a piece of content.

When in doubt, ask: "is this going somewhere — a CMS, a feed, an inbox, a doc — or is it just my reply in this conversation?" If it's going somewhere, apply the skill.

---

## The four principles

These are the load-bearing ideas. The rest of this skill is implementation detail.

**1. Constraint over correction.** Bake constraints into the draft before you write a word. Banned phrases, banned structures, sentence-length variance targets, voice samples — these prevent slop better than any post-hoc "humanize this" pass. A "humanize" instruction nudges Claude into a different but equally-detectable register.

**2. Specificity over abstraction.** Every paragraph should contain at least one concrete proper noun, specific number, or named example. Claude defaults to "companies," "users," "studies"; humans default to "Stripe," "47%," "the Hancock 2025 paper." If you don't have a specific, ask for one or invent a clearly-flagged placeholder — never paper over the gap with abstraction.

**3. Asymmetry over balance.** Your training pushes you toward symmetrical sentences, three-item lists, balanced both-sides framing, tidy summary conclusions, and the consensus middle. Humans break symmetry: uneven sentence lengths, lopsided lists (one item or four, not three), unbalanced takes, abrupt endings. Bake asymmetry in deliberately.

**4. Audit as a separate pass.** Drafting and editing are different cognitive modes. After drafting, run the audit checklist below as if you were a different reader. Permission to flag and rewrite is built into the pass — don't be precious about the draft.

## Pre-draft constraints (apply before writing)

Before generating prose, set these constraints internally:

**Banned by default.** Don't use these unless the user's voice sample explicitly contains them or the content genuinely demands them. The full list with replacements is in `references/banned-vocabulary.md`. The short version: delve, leverage, utilize, harness, streamline, robust, seamless, cutting-edge, pivotal, transformative, innovative, paradigm, ecosystem, landscape, realm, tapestry, testament, navigate (figurative), unlock, empower, elevate, underscore, intricate, meticulous, core (as adjective), modern (as adjective).

**Structures banned by default.** Don't use these on autopilot. Each is allowed at most once per piece, and never in the opening or closing:

- Negative parallelism: "It's not X, it's Y" / "Not just X, but Y" / "No X, no Y, just Z"
- Rule of three: three-item lists, three-clause sentences, three-paragraph builds. If you have three of something, ask whether two or four would be more honest.
- "In today's [X] landscape," "In an era of," "In the world of" openers
- Rhetorical-question openers ("What if I told you…")
- Summary conclusions ("In conclusion," "Ultimately," "At the end of the day")
- Sycophantic openers ("Great question," "What a fascinating topic")

**Formatting banned by default for prose.** Reserve markdown for content that genuinely needs it (technical docs, reference material, instructions). For articles, essays, marketing copy, social posts, and email:

- No bullet lists unless presenting genuinely discrete items
- No H2/H3 headers unless the piece is long enough to need navigation (>1,500 words)
- No bolding except for occasional genuine emphasis
- No emoji unless the user's voice uses them — and never ✅ 🧠 🔥 🔹 as decoration
- No "Key takeaways" or "TL;DR" boxes unless the user requested them

**Sentence-length variance.** Aim for a deliberate mix: some 3–6 word fragments, some 25+ word sentences, the rest in between. If 80% of your sentences land in the 12–22 word range, you're writing AI.

**Voice handling.** If the user provided a voice sample (their writing, a brand voice doc, a style guide), match its rhythm, sentence-length variation, vocabulary range, and rhetorical posture — not just its topics. If they didn't provide one, ask whether they want a specific voice or are happy with a neutral one. Don't invent a voice and assume it's theirs.

**Specificity floor.** Before writing, list the concrete details you'll use: real numbers, named people, named companies, named places, dates, specific products. If the list is empty, the piece will sound generic no matter what else you do. Ask the user for specifics or flag clearly where placeholders live.

## Drafting moves that read as human

Positive techniques that consistently make prose feel less generated:

- **Lead with a claim or a specific, not a frame.** Don't set up the topic; start inside it.
- **Use "says" for attributions.** Claude defaults to "notes," "explains," "highlights," "emphasizes." Humans use "says" most of the time and let speakers' actual words do the work.
- **Allow run-ons and fragments.** Russell et al. (ACL 2025) found humans use ungrammatical constructions more than AI does. A deliberate fragment. Or a sentence that runs on past where you'd expect it to stop because the thought hasn't finished yet.
- **Don't always close the loop.** End at the last interesting sentence, not at the summary. A piece can stop without a bow on it.
- **Pick off-center words.** Claude's sampling pulls toward the safe middle of the distribution. When two synonyms are roughly equivalent, prefer the one that's more specific, more physical, or more particular to the speaker.
- **Let the stance be lopsided.** If the topic has a clear answer, say it. Both-sides framing on every claim reads as AI hedging.
- **Concrete over abstract every time.** "A Series B fintech in Atlanta" beats "a growing financial company." "47% of respondents" beats "many respondents." "The Toyota Camry your dad drove" beats "a reliable vehicle."
- **One specific anecdote beats five general claims.** Especially for marketing and social.

## The post-draft audit (run on every draft before delivering)

After drafting, walk this checklist. For any item that fires, fix it before delivering.

1. **Banned vocab sweep.** Search the draft for the words listed in `references/banned-vocabulary.md`. Replace each with something more specific, more concrete, or simply cut it.
2. **Negative-parallelism count.** How many "It's not X, it's Y" / "Not just X, but Y" / "No X, no Y" structures? Allow at most one in the entire piece, and never in the opening or closing.
3. **Rule-of-three count.** How many three-item lists or three-clause sentences? If more than two, convert at least one to a different number (one, two, or four) or to flowing prose.
4. **Sentence-length variance.** Skim the sentences. If they all feel medium-length, rewrite at least three to be 3–6 words and at least one to be 30+ words.
5. **Specificity audit.** Circle every abstraction (companies, users, customers, businesses, teams, professionals, organizations, individuals, stakeholders). Replace at least half with named or numerated specifics.
6. **Opener test.** Does the first sentence start with "In today's," "In an era of," "In the world of," a flattery line, a rhetorical question, or a generic scene-set? Cut and start with a claim or a specific.
7. **Closer test.** Is there a tidy summary paragraph that restates the points? Cut it. End at the last interesting sentence.
8. **Quote handling test.** If there are quotes, do they all sound similar in register? Are they all attributed with "notes" or "explains"? Use "says" by default and let speakers sound different from the surrounding prose.
9. **Emoji and formatting audit.** Strip ✅ 🧠 🔥 🔹 unless the user uses them. Strip bold except for genuine emphasis. Strip headers from pieces under 1,500 words unless the user requested structure.
10. **The search-engine test.** Could any paragraph appear in the top three Google results for this topic? If yes, rewrite it with more specific language or a less-obvious angle.
11. **The opening-25% rule.** Even if the rest is fine, rewrite the first paragraph more aggressively. Lead sentences carry disproportionate AI signal — Bitton et al. (2025) showed classifiers can identify the source LLM from just the first few tokens. The opener has to feel hand-built.
12. **Read-aloud test.** Mentally read the piece aloud. Anywhere you stumble, a reader will too. Anywhere it sounds like a podcast intro or a corporate webpage, rewrite.

If five or more items fire on the audit, the piece needs a structural rewrite, not just edits. Restart the opener, restructure the middle, and cut the conclusion.

## Format-specific notes

Different formats have different default-failure modes. Apply these on top of the general rules.

### Blog posts and articles
The classic AI failure mode is over-structuring: H2/H3 headers every 200 words, bulleted "key takeaways," symmetrical 3-sentence paragraphs, a summary conclusion. Default to flowing prose. Reserve headers for pieces over 1,500 words and only at genuine section breaks. End abruptly — no "In conclusion."

### Marketing copy and landing pages
The failure mode is inflated language and parallelism. Replace adjectives like *revolutionary, transformative, seamless, robust, innovative* with specific claims. Replace negative parallelism ("It's not just software, it's a movement") with one concrete benefit done well. Use real numbers ("ships in 4 hours") over abstract benefits ("ships fast"). One specific claim beats three generic ones.

### Technical writing and documentation
The failure mode is anthropomorphization ("the system understands…"), elegant variation (using three different terms for the same thing), and fabricated specifics (made-up flags, endpoints, version numbers). Use consistent terminology, short imperative sentences, no contractions in formal docs. Verify every API surface, command, and version number — never invent. Anthropomorphize sparingly. The Google Developer Style Guide is the de facto reference; follow it unless the user has their own.

### Social posts (LinkedIn, X, Threads)
The failure mode is "broetry": fragmented one-line paragraphs, hook-line clichés ("I'll let you in on a secret," "Most people [X]. Smart people [Y]"), three-line setup-pivot-payoff, hashtag stuffing, the brain emoji. Write conversationally, one specific anecdote, one specific number, no parallelism crutch. Lowercase is fine. Imperfection is fine. A post can end mid-thought.

### Email and outreach
The failure mode is template-shaped openers: "I hope this email finds you well," "I was impressed by," "I was fascinated by," "Following up:" — these read as AI even when humans write them. Replace with an actual specific reference (a real recent thing the person did or said). Lowercase subject lines often outperform title case. One specific ask, not three. Allow a sentence fragment or an "And" opener.

### Fiction
The failure mode is what Sam Kriss called "the universal omniwriter": ghosts, quiet, hum, humming, echo, liminal, whisper, shadow, sensory metaphors stuck to abstractions ("grief tasted of metal," "Thursday tasted of almost-Friday"), generic character names (Elara Voss, Kael, Emily Carter, Sarah Thompson), mid-sentence self-questioning. If a character name appears in the draft, swap it for something less default. Cut sensory metaphors attached to immaterial nouns. Don't let everything be "spectral."

## What to do if the user pushes back

If the user says the result sounds too plain, too short, or "missing something" — don't reach for AI defaults. The instinct is usually to add structure (headers, bullets, summary), inflate vocabulary, or add a tidy conclusion. Resist. Instead ask what specifically feels missing: more depth on a section, more specifics, a different angle, a different emotional register. Then add that one thing.

If the user explicitly asks for the things this skill avoids ("can you add a TL;DR at the top," "give me three bullet points," "include a strong CTA"), do what they asked. The skill's defaults are for cases where the user hasn't specified.
